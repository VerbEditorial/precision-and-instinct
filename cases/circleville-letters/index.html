February 7, 1983 · Scioto-Darby Road and Five Points Pike, near Circleville, Ohio

The Circleville Letters

Unresolved Paul Freshour was convicted of attempted murder in 1983 for the booby trap. No one has ever been charged with writing the Circleville letters, and who wrote them remains officially unresolved. This page does not settle that question, and neither did the episode. Every claim is tiered by how well established it is.

On February 7, 1983, a school bus driver outside Circleville, Ohio, stopped to tear down a hand-lettered sign and found a loaded pistol rigged to fire. It was the latest turn in an anonymous letter campaign that had already run for years. A man went to prison for the trap. The letters kept coming. The hosts split on whether the handwriting or the person points to him.

Spring 1977

The mailbox

Beginning in the spring of 1977, anonymous handwritten letters began arriving in mailboxes around Circleville, Ohio. The first ones accused school bus driver Mary Gillispie and school superintendent Gordon Massie of having an affair. No source has ever established that the affair was real. It was the letters’ accusation, nothing more.

  • The writingBlock capitals, no return address, mostly postmarked Columbus
  • The reachHundreds of recipients over nearly two decades, including Ron Gillispie, other residents, local businesses, government offices and newspapers
  • The first targetBus driver Mary Gillispie, accused in the letters of an affair that no source has shown was real
  • The endThe letters stopped arriving in 1994
  1. 01 / 04 · 1983, THE TRAP

    At about 3:30 p.m. on February 7, 1983, Mary Gillispie found a hand-lettered sign with an obscene remark about her daughter at a crossroads on her bus route. She pulled it down and put the sign, its board and a small box on the bus. At home she opened the glued box and found a loaded .25-caliber pistol, rigged to fire when the sign was pulled. The gun was traced to her brother-in-law, Paul Freshour. He said it had been stolen weeks earlier and failed police polygraph testing. His wife, Karen Sue, who was divorcing him, told police she had found torn-up letter drafts in their home.

  2. 02 / 04 · 1983, THE TRIAL

    Freshour’s attempted-murder trial began on October 24, 1983. He was convicted and received the maximum, 7 to 25 years. He was never charged with writing the letters. Some of the handwriting samples he gave the sheriff were copied from actual letters and others were dictated. The defense’s own examiner set the copied pages aside, saw signs of disguise, and found nothing pointing to a different writer, but gave no flat identification.

  3. 03 / 04 · 1983–1994, THE LETTERS CONTINUE

    Anonymous letters kept arriving throughout his imprisonment, some addressed to him. Prison officials said that at times he had no pen or paper and was searched repeatedly. How the letters continued has never been officially explained. He was paroled in 1994, the year the letters stopped.

  4. 04 / 04 · 2021, TWO EXPERTS

    CBS’s 48 Hours revisited the case. Forensic document examiner Beverley East compared 49 letters with Freshour’s known writing and concluded one person wrote them, Freshour. Former FBI profiler Mary Ellen O’Toole reviewed 98 letters and doubted he was the writer, calling the author “not a highly educated person.” Freshour held a master’s degree.

The handwriting, or the person

Same case
Eli’s reading

The physical chain and the handwriting point to Freshour.

The gun was his. He said it had been stolen and failed police polygraph testing, and torn-up drafts of letters turned up in his home. A document examiner later tied the handwriting to him across the years, and Eli points out that even the defense’s own examiner found nothing pointing to a different writer. Eli says plainly that he cannot fully explain the letters that arrived while Freshour was in prison, even with the fingerprint reports, and that he cannot explain away everything else either.

Simone’s reading

The person in the letters is not the man they convicted.

Some of the handwriting samples were copied from the very letters being matched, the defense’s own examiner never said flat out it was him, and the sheriff’s claim that Freshour admitted writing some of the letters has no recording behind it. A former FBI profiler read the writer as less educated than Freshour, who held a master’s degree. The letters kept coming while he was locked up. Simone does not claim to know who wrote them. She is not convinced it was him.

Whether Freshour wrote the letters has never been proven, and he was never charged with it. The two experts worked from overlapping, but not identical, sets of letters, and Beverley East’s methodology was not independently verified. How letters continued during his imprisonment is unexplained. Reports of Freshour’s fingerprints on letters mailed after his arrest differ (about a dozen versus 26, a window that includes about eight months he was out on bail), were not raised at trial, and the underlying lab reports have not been independently reviewed. The sheriff’s account of a partial admission has no recording and no trace in the trial record. Ron Gillispie’s 1977 death was ruled an accident; family members questioned that, and no official reopening has been located. The Gillispie–Massie affair appears only as the letters’ accusation.
