February 22 – May 3, 1946 · Texarkana, Texas and Arkansas

The Town That Dreaded Sundown

Unresolved No one was ever charged with these murders, and the case remains officially unsolved. Youell Swinney was never charged and denied involvement until his death in 1994. What this page says about him is what the record shows or what investigators believed, not a finding of guilt. The case includes the deaths of teenagers. This page does not settle who the Phantom was, and neither did the episode. Every claim is tiered by how well established it is.

Between February 22 and May 3, 1946, four attacks in and around Texarkana left five people dead and three wounded. Three took place on lovers' lanes. The fourth came through a closed farmhouse window, with a different gun. A tan work shirt found among a car thief's belongings and a 21-year-old wife who accused him and took it back are the heart of the case against him. The hosts split on whether the farmhouse was ever the Phantom's.

February 22, 1946

The lovers' lanes

Texarkana is one town laid across two states, half in Texas and half in Arkansas. On February 22, 1946, a masked man in a hood walked up to a couple parked on a back road outside town. He hit Jimmy Hollis over the head with a pistol hard enough to fracture his skull and attacked Mary Jeanne Larey. Both survived. Nobody connected it to anything else yet.

  • The townOne town across two states, Texarkana, Texas, and Texarkana, Arkansas
  • The attacksFour attacks between February 22 and May 3, 1946: five people dead, three wounded
  • The nameTexarkana Gazette editors began calling the attacker “the Phantom” in mid-April, after the third attack
  • The panicBy May 5, forty-seven officers were working the case, and stores sold out of locks, guns and window coverings within a day
  1. 01 / 04 · MARCH AND APRIL, THE LANES

    On March 24, 1946, Richard Griffin, 29, and Polly Ann Moore, 17, were found shot dead in Griffin’s car on a lane off Highway 67, each shot once in the back of the head with a .32-caliber pistol. On April 14, Betty Jo Booker, 15, left a dance at the VFW club with Paul Martin about 1:30 a.m. Martin was found shot dead at about 6:30 a.m., and Booker about five hours later, roughly two miles away. The same .32 pistol was used in both double killings.

  2. 02 / 04 · MAY 3, THE FARMHOUSE

    Shortly before 9 p.m., Virgil Starks, 37, who farmed and ran a welding shop, was shot twice in the back of the head through a closed window of his farmhouse while he sat reading. His wife, Katie, came to see what had happened and was shot twice in the face through the same window. She survived and reached a neighbor’s house on foot. The weapon was a .22, not a .32. The FBI Laboratory concluded the cartridge cases came from “an auto loading weapon similar to the caliber .22 Colt Woodsman pistol.”

  3. 03 / 04 · THE SHIRT

    After Peggy Swinney’s arrest on June 28, 1946, an FBI agent and an Arkansas state police officer searched the couple’s lodging and found a tan work shirt. At the FBI Laboratory on July 15, an examiner slit open the pockets and found sand, magnetic slag balls and brown human hair in the seams, and was asked whether the faded name inside the collar read STARK or STARR. The laboratory could not say how long the name had been there. It called the slag “very significant” but said the sand comparison would “probably carry very little weight.” Some of the hair was similar to Virgil Starks’s and “could not have come from the suspect.”

  4. 04 / 04 · PEGGY SWINNEY’S STATEMENTS

    Peggy Swinney, 21, was questioned by at least a dozen officers, by the FBI’s count. On July 25 she told the sheriff she was with Youell Swinney when he killed Martin and Booker, and she signed a statement on July 26. On July 28 she said the story was untrue and that she had made it because she had been “questioned so extensively.” She signed another statement on February 19, 1947, and wrote her parents the same day that Swinney “was not the one.” The FBI agents wrote that it was hard to see how she knew what she knew unless she had been there, and also that she might have picked it up from the officers who had questioned her.

One Phantom, or two

Same case
Eli’s reading

The FBI’s own file ties the attacks together, and the shirt belongs in it.

Eli reads the file as one killer. He gives up two things along the way: the farmhouse weapon was a pistol, not the rifle most tellings say, and the slag does not say the shirt came from Starks’s shop, only that it came from welding or grinding. What he keeps is the shirt, with hair in its seams that was similar to Virgil Starks’s and could not have come from Swinney, and a witness who walked investigators to a spot she could hardly have known. He says he cannot unhear where she took them.

Simone’s reading

The town had one label, and stuck it on two different crimes.

Simone reads the farmhouse as its own crime: a different gun, a married couple at home, shot through a window, and a town so frightened that any gunshot within fifty miles was going to be the Phantom’s. She gives up the idea that the shirt can be explained away, saying she cannot explain how hair like Virgil Starks’s ended up in the pocket of a shirt in Swinney’s room. She does not call Peggy a liar. She says a frightened 21-year-old asked the same question a dozen ways will eventually give the answer wanted.

Whether Youell Swinney was the Phantom has never been established. He was never charged with the murders and denied them until his death in 1994. Sources disagree on whether one person committed all four attacks. The shirt’s collar name could not be settled as STARK or STARR. Katie Starks first said the shirt was her husband’s, then said she could not be sure. Peggy Swinney’s fingerprints, like her husband’s, were compared with the latent prints without an identification. The 1948 suicide note of H.B. Tennison confessing to the Martin–Booker and Starks killings was not corroborated.
