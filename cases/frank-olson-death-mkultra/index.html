November 19–28, 1953 · Deep Creek Lake, Maryland and New York City

The Mysterious Death of Frank Olson: CIA, LSD, and a Hotel Window

Unresolved No one has ever been charged in connection with Frank Olson’s death, and whether he jumped or was pushed has never been resolved. The Manhattan District Attorney’s inquiry closed in 2001 with no charges, and a federal judge dismissed the family’s lawsuit in 2013. The CIA has described the death as a drug-related suicide. What this page says about the CIA officers is what the record shows or what the family has alleged, not a finding of guilt. Every claim is tiered by how well established it is.

In November 1953 the CIA secretly dosed a bottle of Cointreau with LSD at a retreat in western Maryland. Nine days later, Army bacteriologist Frank Olson went through the window of Room 1018A at the Hotel Statler in New York, sharing the room with CIA chemist Robert Lashbrook. His family was not told about the drug for 22 years. The hosts split on whether the paper record shows a drugged man coming apart or an account written by the people who drugged him.

November 28, 1953

Room 1018A

Olson died on November 28, 1953, after going through the window of Room 1018A at the Hotel Statler in New York. He shared the room with Robert Lashbrook, a CIA chemist and Gottlieb’s deputy. Lashbrook told police he woke to a noise and found the window broken, and did not tell them about the LSD. The hotel later became the Hotel Pennsylvania and was demolished in 2022–2023.

  • The manFrank Olson, a bacteriologist and branch chief in the Special Operations Division at Camp Detrick
  • The roomRoom 1018A, shared with CIA chemist Robert Lashbrook

  1. 01 / 04 · THE WEEK, BY RUWET

    In his written statement of December 1, 1953, Olson’s boss and friend Lt. Col. Vincent Ruwet recorded Olson’s decline day by day: agitated the morning after, asking whether he should be fired or quit, feeling “all mixed up,” taken to New York for help, found in the hotel lobby before dawn on Thanksgiving saying he had thrown away his wallet and ID, and asking on the drive home to be turned over to the police. Ruwet also records that Olson had asked to be home for Thanksgiving dinner, and that on the phone on Friday night, November 27, he sounded relaxed. In New York Olson was taken to Dr. Harold Abramson, an allergist and immunologist rather than a psychiatrist, who held CIA contracts for LSD research.

  2. 02 / 04 · NOVEMBER 28, ROOM 1018A

    Olson died on November 28, 1953, after going through the window of Room 1018A at the Hotel Statler, which he shared with CIA chemist Robert Lashbrook, Gottlieb’s deputy. Lashbrook told police he woke to a noise and found the window broken. He did not tell them about the LSD. The record disagrees on the floor (tenth or thirteenth) and on the time of the fall: about 2:30 a.m. in a 1975 Justice Department summary, 2:45 in Lashbrook’s later account, before 2 according to a hotel employee. Ruwet records that Gottlieb telephoned him with the news at about 2:45.

  3. 03 / 04 · 1994 TO 2001, THE RE-EXAMINATION

    In 1994 Olson’s son Eric had his father’s body exhumed. Forensic scientist James Starrs led a re-examination and concluded that a blow to the head came before the fall, calling the findings “rankly and starkly suggestive of homicide.” The Manhattan District Attorney reopened the case in 1996, questioned Lashbrook in 1998 under a court order, and closed the inquiry in 2001 with no charges. Prosecutor Steve Saracco told the Baltimore Sun, “We could never prove it was murder.”

  4. 04 / 04 · 1975 TO 2013, THE PAPER

    The family was not told about the LSD until 1975, when the Rockefeller Commission reported that the CIA had given LSD to people who did not know it and that one person died in 1953, “apparently as a result.” A July 11, 1975 White House memo shows Ford’s aides recommending an expression of regret and warning that a lawsuit or hearing might require disclosing “highly classified national security information.” Ford apologized to the family on July 21, and in 1976 Congress paid them $750,000 in full settlement. In 2013 Judge James Boasberg dismissed the sons’ suit on three grounds, writing that “the public record supports many of the allegations that follow, farfetched as they may sound.”

Jumped or pushed

Same case
Eli’s reading

A drugged man came apart, and nobody has ever put a second man in the room.

Eli reads the record as a breakdown. A man dosed without warning spent days telling the people around him he was coming apart, no witness or evidence places a third person in the room, Lashbrook’s account stayed the same from 1975 to 1998, and the Manhattan DA’s office closed its inquiry without charges. He concedes the head wound is not cleanly explained by a breakdown story, and that the record cannot agree on the floor or the time, which he calls the ordinary sloppiness of 1953 records.

Simone’s reading

The paper is theirs, and the paper doesn’t agree with itself.

Simone distrusts the record because most of it was written by the institution that drugged him. She points to Starrs’s 1994 finding of a head wound before the fall, the disagreement over the floor and the time of the fall, and the agency’s 22 years of silence. She concedes she cannot say who would have pushed him or why, and that a prosecutor’s “we could never prove it” is not a finding that it wasn’t murder.

Whether Frank Olson jumped or was pushed has never been established. No one was charged. Sources disagree on who physically spiked the Cointreau (John Marks’s 1979 history says Gottlieb; the family’s 2012 lawsuit alleged Lashbrook), on the floor, and on the time of the fall. The case for homicide rests on Starrs’s 1994 finding, the reported absence of glass cuts and doubts about the CIA’s own record. The case against rests on the 2001 close of the DA inquiry, Olson’s documented paranoia after the dosing, Lashbrook’s consistent account and the absence of any evidence of a third person in the room. Several details the hosts discuss rest on a single account and are marked that way on the source list.
