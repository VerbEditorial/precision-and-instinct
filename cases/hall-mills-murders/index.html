September 14–16, 1922 · De Russey’s Lane, New Brunswick, New Jersey

The Hall–Mills Murders

Unsolved · Acquitted No one was ever convicted. Frances Hall, Henry Stevens and William “Willie” Stevens were tried and acquitted in December 1926, and charges against a fourth suspect were dropped before he was tried. This page does not resolve the case, and neither did the episode. Every claim is tiered by how well established it is.

On September 16, 1922, two bodies were found laid out side by side under a crab apple tree in a New Jersey lane. Four years later a family was tried for it and acquitted. After a century the case is not short of evidence. It is short of evidence anyone can trust. The hosts split on what the acquittal means.

November 3 – December 3, 1926

A witness on a hospital bed

Jane Gibson, a local farmer the press nicknamed “the Pig Woman” because she raised hogs, was the prosecution’s central eyewitness. She testified from a hospital bed wheeled into the Somerville courtroom, describing a woman’s scream of “Don’t!” and a shouted name, “Henry.” Her account had shifted across her earlier statements.

  • The witnessJane Gibson, “the Pig Woman,” testifying from a hospital bed
  • The trialSomerville, N.J., Nov. 3 – Dec. 3, 1926
  • The pressRoughly 300 reporters, including Damon Runyon and H.L. Mencken
  • The defendantsFrances Hall, Henry Stevens and Willie Stevens
  1. 01 / 04 · 1922, THE SCENE

    Hall, 41, and Mills, 34, were found September 16 under a crab apple tree, laid side by side, with torn love letters scattered around them and Hall’s calling card at his feet.

  2. 02 / 04 · 1922–23, THE FIRST INVESTIGATION

    The scene was never secured. Two counties argued over jurisdiction while crowds took souvenirs. No one was indicted, and evidence and grand jury paperwork later went missing.

  3. 03 / 04 · 1926, THE TRIAL

    Willie Stevens’s fingerprint was on Hall’s calling card, and he owned a .32 pistol with its firing mechanism filed down. Henry Stevens’s alibi had three witnesses.

  4. 04 / 04 · DECEMBER 3, 1926, AND AFTER

    All three were acquitted. The case remains unsolved. Every named principal was dead by 1992, and no DNA testing has been reported.

Doubt or a family that walked

Same case
Eli’s reading

Reasonable doubt is the honest read.

The scene was compromised within hours, the star witness couldn’t keep her story straight, and a fingerprint proves presence, not murder. Henry’s alibi holds. He thinks the jury got it right, and he calls the case one built on the shape of guilt rather than the fact of it.

Simone’s reading

The pattern is hard to wave off.

A humiliated wife, brothers with every reason to protect the family name, and a fingerprint on the card: innocent explanation or not, it’s still his hand on it. She argues reasonable doubt did a lot of quiet work for a family with money and standing, and she thinks the family walked out with something they didn’t deserve.

Several details in the episode rest on a single account and are left out of the clips: Ralph Gorsline’s presence in the lane and his admission that he heard shots around 10:15, Gibson’s growing detail over four years, her mule “Jenny,” and the stenographer and word-count figures. The scratches on Frances Hall’s cheek were never corroborated to the standard of the fingerprint or the pistol. Historians split: Gerald Tomlinson (1999) concluded the Stevens siblings were guilty, while William Kunstler concluded the acquittal was probably right.
